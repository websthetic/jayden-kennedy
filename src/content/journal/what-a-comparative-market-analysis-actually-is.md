An automated valuation looks at what sold nearby and divides by square footage. It cannot see that the house three doors down was gutted in 2019, that yours backs onto a school, or that the two properties on your street that failed to sell last spring were both priced from the same bad assumption.

A comparative market analysis is the same question answered by someone who has walked the street. I read the last ninety days: everything that sold, everything that expired, and everything that is sitting. Expired listings are the most useful of the three, because they mark the ceiling — the number the market has already refused.

Then the adjustments. A finished basement is not worth its cost. A third bathroom is worth more than a fourth bedroom in most of Durham. A renovation done eight years ago counts as maintenance, not an upgrade. Each adjustment gets written down with the reason next to it, so you can disagree with any single line without throwing out the whole number.

![Jayden Kennedy holding a sold sign](/assets/images/cabinets.jpg)

What comes back to you is a range rather than a figure, the evidence for each end of it, and the conditions that would move it — a rate change, two more listings on the street, a slow April.

It takes an afternoon and I do it by hand. That is not a selling point, it is the only way the adjustments are defensible when an offer is on the table and someone asks where the number came from.

By the time an offer is written, the comparables are already read.
