Ask ten people how a house makes money and nine will say the price goes up. Appreciation is real, but it is the last of four mechanisms and the only one you have no hand in.

The first is amortisation. Every payment you make is split between interest and principal, and the principal half is savings you have been forced to make. In the first year of a [amount] mortgage at [rate], about [figure] of what leaves your account each month stays yours. That share grows every year you hold, without you doing anything.

The second is leverage. You buy the whole asset with a fraction of the money. A five percent move on a property you own outright returns five percent. The same move on a property bought with twenty percent down returns twenty-five percent on the cash you put in. Leverage works in both directions, which is the part the seminar circuit leaves out, and it is why the carrying cost matters more than the purchase price.

The third is income. Either rent from a tenant, or the rent you stop paying to somebody else. Both are taxed differently, which is worth an hour with an accountant.

The fourth is appreciation, and it is the one that gets written about. Over a long enough hold it has been reliable in this region. Over three years it is a coin toss, and a coin toss is not a plan.

None of this is secret and none of it needs a course. What it needs is a number you can defend: what the property costs to hold, what it returns while you hold it, and what happens to both if rates move two points against you.

That is the conversation I have before we look at a single home.
